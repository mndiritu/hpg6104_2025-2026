Epidemiologic Study Designs

Strengths, Biases, and Causal Utility

01

The Causal Lens

We don't just ask "which design is better?" We ask: "Under what assumptions does this design allow a valid causal inference?"

Exchangeability

Comparison groups must have the same outcome distribution if exposure were removed.

Temporality

The essential criterion: the cause must precede the effect.

02

Comparative Summary of Designs

Design Causal Utility Primary Bias Risk Key Measures
Cross-Sectional Low Reverse causation, Survival bias Prev. Ratio (PR)
Case-Control Medium Recall, Selection, Collider bias Odds Ratio (OR)
Cohort High Loss to Follow-up (LTFU) RR, HR, IR
RCT Very High Attrition, Non-compliance RR, RD, NNT
03

Beyond the Individual

Ecological Fallacy

The error of inferring individual-level associations from group-level correlations (e.g., country-level sugar consumption vs diabetes). Confounding at the group level makes these unsuitable for causal claims.

Multilevel Modeling (ICC)

Accounts for people nested in environments (schools, clinics). The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) tells us how much context matters: ICC > 0.10 indicates substantial clustering requiring multilevel analysis.

Summary Memo

  • There is no perfect design—only deliberate compromise.
  • Always assess Temporality: Did the exposure precede the outcome?
  • In Case-Control studies, define your Source Population strictly to avoid selection bias.
  • Use DAGs to decide which variables to adjust for—and which (colliders) to avoid.
