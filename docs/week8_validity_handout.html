Bias and Validity

The Systematic Foundations of Epidemiological Rigour

01

Bias (Systematic Error) vs. Random Error

Feature Bias (Systematic Error) Random Error
Direction Consistent directional distortion. Non-directional, chance variation.
Sample Size Effect NOT reduced by larger N. Reduced (averages out) as N increases.
Quantification Requires quantitative bias analysis. Confidence intervals, p-values.

"A study of 100,000 participants can be more biased than a study of 500 if the design is flawed."

02

The Bias Taxonomy

Selection Bias

Errors in who enters or remains in the study. The relationship in the sample differs from the source population.

  • Healthy Worker Bias: Workers tend to be healthier than general pop.
  • Berkson's Bias: Hospitalised controls are not representative.
  • Attrition Bias: Non-random loss to follow-up.

Information Bias

Systematic errors in measuring exposure or outcome, leading to misclassification.

  • Recall Bias: Differential memory search (classic in Case-Control).
  • Observer Bias: Assessor knowledge affects measurement.
  • Hawthorne Effect: Behavior changes because participants are watched.
03

Validity: Internal vs. External

Internal Validity

Does the study accurately reflect the true association within the study population? Necessary precondition for any scientific inference.

External Validity (Generalisability)

Can the findings be applied to settings or contexts outside the original study? Critical for transportability of Western evidence to African public health contexts.

04

Quantitative Bias Analysis: The E-value

# How strong would a hidden confounder need to be?

E-value = RR + √(RR * (RR - 1))

If an observational study reports an OR of 2.0, the E-value is 3.41. This means a hidden confounder needs an RR of at least 3.41 with both exposure and outcome to fully explain away the result.
